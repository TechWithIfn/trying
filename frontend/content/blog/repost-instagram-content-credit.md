Short answer: ask the creator first, get a yes in writing, then repost with a visible tag, a mention in the caption, and a link back. Credit alone — without permission — is not enough.

Reposting is the handshake of Instagram. Done right, both accounts grow and a working relationship starts. Done wrong, it is just theft with a tag attached. The difference is smaller than most people think, and it comes down to one word: permission.

## Credit is courtesy, permission is the rule

Here is the misunderstanding that causes most repost drama: tagging someone is not the same as having their okay. Credit says "this is theirs." Permission says "they said I could use it." You need the second one; the first is just manners on top.

Think of it like borrowing a bike. Pointing at the owner while you ride off is not borrowing. Asking, hearing yes, and then saying thanks loudly — that is borrowing.

## How to ask a creator for repost permission

Keep it short and specific. Creators ignore vague messages and answer clear ones. Say who you are, exactly which post you mean, where you will repost it, and whether the repost is commercial. Here is a template that works:

> Hi! I run [@youraccount], a page about [topic]. I'd love to repost your [photo/reel] from [date] about [subject] to my feed, with full credit and a tag. It would stay up permanently / for one week. Would that be okay?

Notice what is in there: the exact post, the exact use, the exact duration. The easier you make it to say yes, the more yeses you get. Small accounts often agree immediately; bigger ones may ask for payment, which is fair — their work has value.

Timing matters too. Message when the creator is active — shortly after they post, not at 3 a.m. — and never open with the ask in a comment thread. A DM (or the business email in their bio for anything commercial) shows you respect the negotiation enough to hold it privately. One polite follow-up after several days is fine; daily nudges get you blocked.

## What proper credit looks like

Once you have permission, make the credit impossible to miss. Tag the creator on the image or video itself, mention them in the first line of your caption (not buried after twenty hashtags), and link back to the original post where the platform allows it. Write one line about why you are sharing it — a repost with context performs better and feels respectful rather than extractive.

What proper credit is not: "credit to owner," "DM for credit," or a tag hidden where nobody will tap it. If a stranger could not find the creator from your post in five seconds, the credit has failed.

## What never counts: cropping watermarks, "credit to owner"

Two things poison a repost instantly. Cropping out a watermark or signature tells the creator you value their work but not their name — it is the fastest way to turn a compliment into a dispute. And "credit to owner" or "DM for credit/removal" is not a legal shield; it is an admission you did not ask. Takedown requests and platform strikes follow exactly these posts.

If a post has no clear author, that does not make it free. It makes it unusable until you find the source.

Reverse-image search is your friend here: upload the image to a search engine's image lookup and the original often surfaces in seconds, along with the creator's name. For videos, distinctive frames screenshotted and searched the same way frequently lead home. Ten minutes of detective work beats using an orphaned file and meeting its owner later through a takedown notice.

## Reposting stories vs feed posts: different etiquette

Stories have their own norms. Sharing someone's public story to your own story with the built-in share function is expected behavior — Instagram literally built the button for it, and the creator's name travels along automatically. Lifting a story's photo or video out and posting it to your permanent feed is different: that needs the same ask-first treatment as any repost.

Saving a story that mentions you or features your event is fine for your own records. Publishing it elsewhere still needs a yes.

## What to do if someone reposts you without asking

Start friendly — most cases are ignorance, not malice. Comment or message: "Hey, glad you liked it! Could you add a tag / take it down / check with me first next time?" Most people comply immediately. If they refuse or ignore you, use the platform's report tools and, for serious cases, the DMCA process. Keep your originals, drafts and posting dates as proof; they establish the work is yours in minutes.

Document everything from the first message: screenshots of the misuse with dates, your polite request, their response or silence. Platforms and any formal process move faster with a clean timeline than with angry paragraphs. And resist the public callout as a first resort — it feels satisfying and frequently backfires, turning a winnable quiet resolution into a public fight where both sides look bad.

## FAQ

**Is giving credit enough to repost legally?**
No. Credit is good manners, not a license. Only the creator's permission (or an actual license) makes reposting proper.

**What if I can't find who made it?**
Then you cannot use it. An unknown author is not an absent author — keep looking, or pick something else.

**Can I repost if I edit or filter it first?**
Editing does not create permission. A filtered repost without consent is still a repost without consent — plus now you have altered their work.

**What if they said yes but I want to use it differently now?**
Ask again for the new use. Permission covers what was agreed — a feed feature is not ad consent, and a week is not a year.

**Should I keep proof of permission?**
Always. Screenshot the DM or save the email, noting what was agreed. Memories fade; files do not.

**Does this apply to stories too?**
Built-in story sharing is fine and expected. Extracting story media into a permanent post needs permission like anything else.

**What if the creator reposts my repost — who owns that?**
You each own your own layer: they own the original, you own your caption and arrangement. Neither of you gains rights over the other's work through the exchange.
