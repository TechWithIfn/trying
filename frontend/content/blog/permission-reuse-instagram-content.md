Short answer: identify the actual creator, send a short message naming the exact post and use, get the yes in writing, and save it. Most small creators agree within a day when the ask is specific.

Every brand page, meme account and campaign manager eventually needs someone else's photo or clip. The gap between "I found the perfect post" and "I can legally use it" is one conversation — and most people bungle it by being vague, demanding, or silent. A good permission request is a small, learnable skill that pays off for years.

## Figure out exactly what you want to use it for

Before writing a word, answer four questions for yourself: which exact post, where will it appear (your feed, stories, ads, website?), for how long, and will you edit it? "Can I use your photo?" is unanswerable. "Can I post your March 3rd sourdough photo to my bakery's feed for two weeks, unedited, with full credit?" can be answered in seconds.

Specificity signals respect. It tells the creator you have thought about their work rather than mass-messaging fifty accounts.

## Find the actual rights holder

The account that posted it is often — but not always — the owner. Photographers shoot for brands, friends post each other's work, repost pages repost. Check the caption for photo credits, look for watermarks, and if the trail points elsewhere, follow it. Asking the wrong person and hearing yes protects you not at all.

When in doubt, ask the poster directly: "Did you shoot this yourself?" It is a normal question and honest creators answer it plainly. Evasiveness is itself an answer — move on.

## Write a short, specific permission request

Five sentences is plenty: who you are, which post, where and how long, what is in it for them, and a clear yes/no question. Here is a template:

> Hi [name]! I'm [you] from [@account], a [what you do] page. I'd love to use your [photo/reel] from [date] about [subject] [where: in our feed / on our website / in an ad running X weeks]. We'd credit you with [tag + mention + link] and [payment / product / feature in return]. Would you be happy with that?

Send it as a DM or email, not a public comment — negotiations deserve privacy. And send one follow-up after a few days, not five. Silence after two polite messages is a no.

For business use, prefer the email in their bio over DMs — it signals seriousness, lands where they do business, and creates a cleaner paper trail. Include a subject line that states the ask plainly ("Permission request: your March 3rd sourdough photo") so it survives inbox triage and stays findable for both sides later.

## What to offer in return

For casual non-commercial reposts, prominent credit plus genuine engagement is often enough. For anything commercial — ads, product pages, campaigns — expect to pay. Rates vary wildly, but the principle does not: commercial use of someone's work deserves commercial compensation.

Alternatives to cash work when cash is not on the table: free product, a feature to your large audience, a backlink from a strong site, future collaboration. Name the offer explicitly rather than promising "exposure" — creators have heard that word too many times.

## Get it in writing and save the message

A verbal yes evaporates; a written yes endures. DM screenshots, emails, even a confirming text all count — anything showing who agreed to what. After they agree, reply with a one-line summary: "Just to confirm: your March 3rd photo, our feed, two weeks, tagged and linked. Thanks again!" Their confirmation of your summary is your license.

Store permissions in one folder — screenshots, emails, dates. If a dispute ever arises, or a new team member asks "can we use this?", the answer is a file search away instead of a memory.

Name each proof file for its content: `permission-@username-sourdough-march2026.png` beats `Screenshot_1042.png` completely. Pair it with a one-line text note restating the terms (channels, duration, credit given) so nobody has to re-read a whole DM thread to confirm what was agreed. Future audits — platform disputes, brand-safety reviews, team handovers — run on exactly this kind of boring, findable paperwork.

## If they say no or never reply: walk away gracefully

No is a complete answer, and silence after two messages is also an answer. Thank them anyway and find another post — there are millions. Never use the work after a no, never pressure, and never argue about fair use in their DMs. The creator community is small and memory is long; gracious behavior now brings opportunities later.

Keep a shortlist habit for exactly this situation: whenever you find a usable post, find two backups in the same session before asking anyone. If your first choice declines, you pivot the same day instead of stalling the project. Over time the shortlist becomes a roster of friendly creators you have worked with before — and repeat relationships make every future permission faster, warmer and cheaper than cold asks.

## FAQ

**Is a DM "yes" legally enough?**
For most everyday reposts, a clear written yes covering the specific use is solid. For high-value commercial use, a short written agreement is worth the extra step.

**What if the creator is a big account that never replies?**
Try their listed business email rather than DMs. If there is still no answer, treat it as unavailable and move on — unanswered is not consent.

**Can permission be revoked later?**
Generally, yes, for future use — which is another reason to define duration upfront. Honor takedown requests promptly even when you believe you had permission.

**Do I need permission for stories too?**
Built-in story sharing keeps the creator's name attached and is expected behavior. Lifting story media into permanent posts needs permission like anything else.

**What if multiple people appear in the photo?**
Get the photographer's permission for the image, and be aware that recognizable people have their own rights over commercial use of their likeness. For ads, clear both sides.

**How long should I wait for a reply before moving on?**
About a week across two polite messages. Then treat it as unavailable — unanswered is not consent, and patience here protects you later.

**What if they agree verbally in a comment thread?**
Ask them to confirm the same terms in a DM or email. Public comments get deleted, buried, or disputed — a private written yes is the only proof that holds up.
