Short answer: ask each creator for written permission covering where, how long and whether edited — then credit, file the proof, and pay when the use is commercial. Screenshots of praise are not licenses.

User-generated content is marketing gold: real customers, real photos, trust no studio shoot can buy. It is also the fastest way for a brand to end up in a dispute, because "they posted it publicly and tagged us!" is not permission to run it in ads. The brands that do UGC well follow a boring, repeatable process. Here it is.

## Why UGC is gold and why screenshots in DMs aren't permission

A customer's unboxing reel outperforms polished creative because it is believable — that believability is exactly what makes it valuable, and value deserves consent. A tagged post is an invitation to a conversation ("we'd love to feature this — may we?"), not a transfer of rights. Liking, commenting or screenshotting changes nothing legally.

Internal rule worth adopting: no UGC goes live without a filed permission. No exceptions for "small" reposts — small reposts become exhibits just as easily as campaigns.

## Asking customers for reuse rights (templates that get yeses)

Ask in the same place they posted — a comment or DM on the original — so context is obvious. Be specific about the use, keep it short, make yes easy:

> We love this! We're [@brand] and we'd like to feature your photo in our [feed / website gallery / story highlights] for [3 months], credited as @you with a link. OK with you? Reply YES and we'll take that as your permission.

Response rates surprise first-timers: most customers are delighted, especially with credit attached. For ads or paid placements, say so upfront and mention compensation in the first message — discovering commercial use later is how goodwill dies.

Scale the process before you need it. At low volume, DMs plus a spreadsheet suffice. Past a dozen assets a month, move to a simple pipeline: a saved reply template, a dedicated inbox or form for submissions (many brands add "tag us for a chance to be featured" with terms linked), and one owner who tracks permissions. UGC programs die from disorganization, not refusals — build the rails while volume is small.

## What your permission message must cover: where, how long, edited?

Four terms prevent nearly all disputes. Where: feed, stories, website, ads, packaging — name each channel, because permission for a story highlight is not permission for a billboard. How long: a month, a year, ongoing — with an end date you can honor. Edited: cropped, filtered, combined with other assets, or used verbatim — say which. Commercial: organic feature versus paid promotion, stated plainly.

Their "YES" reply to those four stated terms is your license. Anything you did not name, you do not have. When campaigns expand (organic post becomes an ad), re-ask — scope creep without consent is the classic UGC lawsuit shape.

Put special attention on the edit term. Cropping a vertical photo square, color-grading a video, splicing testimonials together, adding your branding over their content — each is a modification the creator did not necessarily agree to. Either secure "light editing for format" consent upfront or show them the final creative before it ships. Creators who see the finished piece become promoters; creators surprised by it become complainants.

## Crediting creators in a way that builds the relationship

Credit visibly: tag on the image, mention in the caption's first lines, link where possible. Then go one step further — notify the creator when it goes live, thank them publicly, engage with their account genuinely. Creators who feel celebrated produce more content and say yes faster next time.

Track performance back to them when you can ("your photo drove our best week!"). Nothing secures a long-term creator relationship like showing your work with numbers.

## Storing permissions where you can find them later

Every permission gets filed the day it arrives: screenshot or email, creator handle, post link, agreed terms, date. A shared folder or simple spreadsheet works — columns for creator, asset, channels, expiry, proof file. Review expiries quarterly; pull anything whose permission lapsed.

This file is also your shield. Platform dispute, creator's changed mind, new team member questioning an asset — the answer is a search, not a scramble. Brands that file survive audits; brands that "remember agreeing" do not.

## When to pay: whitelisting, ads and influencer content

Organic features can run on credit and goodwill. Paid media cannot — whitelisting a creator's post as your ad, boosting UGC with spend, or building campaigns around creator content all demand payment. Rates vary by following, usage and duration; negotiate plainly and put numbers in the same written thread as the permission.

Also pay (or formally license) when usage is prominent: homepage heroes, packaging, out-of-home. The rule scales with the value you extract — the more the asset earns, the more the creator is owed.

Build creator relationships beyond transactions and the economics improve for everyone. Feature creators regularly, pay promptly, tag generously, and share performance wins — creators prioritize brands that treat them as partners, often offering first refusal on new content and friendlier rates over time. The brands with the best UGC pipelines rarely "collect" content at all; they cultivate creators who send it voluntarily. Permission workflows get you compliant; relationships get you supplied.

## FAQ

**Can we repost stories customers tag us in?**
Built-in story sharing (with attribution attached) is expected behavior. Lifting story media into permanent feed posts or ads needs the same written permission as anything else.

**What if a customer deletes their post later?**
Your filed permission still covers your copy, but read its terms — many brands voluntarily pull the asset too, as goodwill. Check whether your agreement addressed deletion.

**Do we need permission if we only show it internally?**
Internal moodboards and private references are low-risk, but anything client-facing, published or used in pitches needs rights. When in doubt, ask — the ask is cheap.

**Can permission be withdrawn?**
Creators can ask you to stop future use; honor takedowns promptly even mid-campaign. Defined durations in your agreements make exits clean for both sides.

**What about content from minors?**
Extra care required: get a parent or guardian's consent alongside the creator's, in writing. Youth content in ads without proper clearance is a high-severity risk — skip it unless paperwork is airtight.

**Can we use UGC in other countries?**
Only if your permission covered those territories. State geographic scope ("worldwide" or named regions) in the original ask to avoid re-clearing every expansion.
