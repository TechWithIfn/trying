Short answer: you own everything you create the moment you create it. A public account lets people look — it does not let them take. And music is the fastest way to get into trouble.

Copyright sounds like a subject for lawyers and record labels. In practice it is the operating system of your creative life: every photo you take, every video you edit, every caption you write is covered from the second it exists. Knowing the basics takes twenty minutes and saves you from both kinds of copyright pain — someone stealing from you, and you accidentally stealing from someone.

## You own your photos and videos the moment you make them

No registration, no watermark, no "©" symbol required. The instant you press the shutter or export the edit, the work is yours. That ownership includes the right to decide who copies it, who posts it, and who profits from it.

This surprises people twice. First, when they learn their casual phone photos are fully protected works. Second, when they learn the reverse: everyone else's casual phone photos are equally protected, including the ones they wanted to "just borrow."

Keep your originals — RAW files, project files, unposted drafts with timestamps. They are your proof of authorship if a dispute ever starts, and proof settles most disputes before they begin.

One more ownership trap: work you commission. If you pay a photographer or editor, default ownership usually stays with them unless your agreement says otherwise — "I paid for it" transfers the files, not necessarily the rights. Put ownership or a clear license in writing before money changes hands, even with friends. The conversation is mildly awkward once and prevents genuinely painful fights later.

## What "public account" does and does not allow

Posting publicly gives Instagram a license to display your work inside Instagram. It does not give strangers a license to do anything else with it. A public post means "come look," not "take one."

What others may do: view, like, comment, share via Instagram's built-in buttons (which keep your name attached). What they may not do without your okay: download and re-upload as their own, print and sell it, use it in their ads, or crop your name off. The visibility of the post changes nothing about who owns it.

## Music and trending sounds: the riskiest material

Here is where most small creators get burned. That trending song behind your reel belongs to artists, labels and publishers — Instagram has deals letting you use it inside Instagram, and those deals end at Instagram's borders. Post the same reel with the same song elsewhere, run it as an ad, or hand it to a brand, and you can face mutes, takedowns and claims.

Practical rule: if the sound is the point (a dance, a lip-sync), keep it on Instagram. If you want the video to travel — other platforms, client work, ads — use music you own, music explicitly licensed for reuse, or original audio you recorded yourself.

Where do you get safe music? Platform audio libraries (YouTube's, Meta's sound collections) for use inside their ecosystems, royalty-free catalogs with clear licenses for wider use, and direct licenses from independent artists — many small musicians license cheaply for creator use and appreciate the exposure when terms are explicit. Whatever the source, save the license text alongside the track file. "I downloaded it from a free site" is not a license; the site's written terms are.

## Fair use in plain language (and why it rarely covers reposting)

Fair use (or fair dealing, in some countries) lets people use small portions of others' work for purposes like criticism, commentary, news and teaching. It is deliberately fuzzy, decided case by case, and it weighs things like how much you took and whether your use replaces the original.

Reposting someone's full photo or full reel to your own feed, even with credit, is close to never fair use — you took all of it, and your post substitutes for theirs. Commentary that shows a work while genuinely discussing it sits on firmer ground, but "firmer ground" still means a gray area a court would have to resolve. When creators ask me if their repost is fair use, my honest answer is usually: if you have to ask, get permission instead.

## Watermarks, timestamps and originals as proof

You do not need a watermark for protection, but visible signatures deter casual theft — many repost accounts skip watermarked work because the credit cannot be cropped away cleanly. More important is invisible proof: original files with metadata, edit project files, and the posting date on your account. Together they establish authorship in minutes.

A quiet tip: email important originals to yourself or keep them in cloud storage with version history. A timestamped copy outside your control is hard to argue with.

## What to do when someone steals your work

Step one is a polite message — most theft is thoughtless, and a direct ask resolves most cases within a day. Step two is a platform report through Instagram's built-in tools. Step three, for stubborn or commercial theft, is a DMCA takedown notice, which platforms are legally required to act on. Keep records of everything: screenshots of the misuse, your originals, dates.

Escalate calmly and in order. The creator who documents politely and proceeds step by step almost always wins; the one who starts with public accusations sometimes becomes the problem.

A final habit that prevents most disputes entirely: publish your repost policy. A story highlight or bio line stating "reposts with credit OK for non-commercial use, DM first for brands" pre-answers the common cases and filters serious requests into your inbox. Creators with clear policies get asked properly far more often — and when someone ignores a stated policy, your enforcement position is visibly stronger. Clarity upstream saves conflict downstream.

## FAQ

**Do I need to register my copyright?**
In most countries, no — protection is automatic. Registration (where available) adds legal advantages for court cases, but for everyday creator life, originals plus timestamps are enough.

**Can someone use my photo if they credit me?**
Not without your permission. Credit without consent is still use without consent — flattering, but not lawful.

**What about memes made from my work?**
Memes live in the grayest area of all. Transformative meme use is sometimes defensible, but straight reposts with a caption slapped on usually are not. Handle case by case, starting with a message.

**Does deleting my post remove others' copies?**
No. Deletion removes your post, not copies others already saved. Another reason to act early when misuse appears.

**I used a trending sound — can my reel be taken down?**
Inside Instagram, licensed trending sounds are generally fine. Problems start when the reel travels off-platform or into ads, where that license does not reach.

**Someone traced my art / copied my format. Is that infringement?**
Style and format are not protected the way exact copies are — being inspired is legal, duplicating is not. The line sits at substantial similarity of the actual work, which is genuinely case-by-case.
